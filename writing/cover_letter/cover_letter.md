We submit our manuscript, “Model-based inference of reward identity in the lateral orbitofrontal cortex,” for consideration as a Research Report in *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*.

Flexible decisions depend on anticipating consequences that have never been directly experienced. Our earlier work in PNAS identified neural representations of anticipated rewards in human orbitofrontal cortex ([Kahnt et al., 2010](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0912838107)) and demonstrated identity-specific coding of future rewards ([Howard et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1503550112)). These findings raise a further question: does inference through separately learned associations recover only a scalar estimate of reward value, or the identity of a specific expected outcome? The present study addresses this question by combining sensory preconditioning, selective outcome devaluation, and functional MRI in humans, extending this line of PNAS research from directly learned reward expectations to model-based inference.

Participants learned associations between visual cues and, separately, between some of those cues and specific food odors. After a meal selectively reduced the desirability of one odor, participants reduced their choices of both directly associated cues and cues whose outcomes had to be inferred through the learned associations. This transfer of devaluation provides behavioral evidence that inference accesses specific outcome information. Multivariate analyses identified representations of inferred reward identity in lateral orbitofrontal cortex. A classifier trained on responses to experienced odors also decoded the inferred odor identity from responses to visual cues, even though no odors were delivered during the probe test. These findings link flexible choice to the reinstatement of representations of specific expected outcomes.

Outcome-identity effects were significant for preconditioned cues but were not significant for directly conditioned cues. To explain this pattern, we propose a resource-limited model that allocates resources to outcome representations according to their computational benefit, providing a testable framework for future experiments.

We believe the manuscript will interest PNAS readers across neuroscience, psychology, and computational approaches to decision making. Beyond identifying the content of model-based inference, the work connects associative learning and cognitive-map theories with the broader question of how limited neural resources are allocated to information useful for behavior.
